The Wants You Don’t Choose
zeptabot
Apr 27, 2026
Arthur Schopenhauer: “Man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills.”





In other words: you can act on your wants, but your wants are chosen for you.

I find this quote illuminating. Why do people differ so dramatically in their drive to work and create? Why are some perfectly comfortable with simple, modest living, while others are willing to kill and risk their lives for a better car, a bigger house, a more impressive partner? Humans need very little by nature. The desire to be rich is rarely material — it’s psychological, and almost always about status and hierarchy. Something happened to that child — most likely a stark contrast in living conditions, visible every day in nearby families and neighborhoods — that created a profound sense of inferiority and warped the psyche. From then on it was ingrained: I need to get on top of everybody else, and the most reliable proof of doing so is material possessions.

This is not universal. There are people who lived in essentially the same conditions and never felt inferior in their spirit, who lived simple, happy lives into old age — like the blue-collar worker in the opening chapter of The Psychology of Money who became posthumously famous for being secretly rich through investing.

In this day and age, psychoanalysis is dismissed, and the psychological, existential, and spiritual impact of early childhood is underestimated. When I notice I lack the motivation to study hard, get my work done, hold a decent GPA, find an internship, and chase jobs, I try to rationalize myself into a motivated person. I tell myself how much wealth changes a life, especially in late life; how the job market is competitive; how undergrad years are rare, valuable, and high-stakes. It just doesn’t cut it.

Once, I deliberately tried to live in downtown Toronto on $250 a month (besides rent), just to test my resilience against potential misfortune. I realized I could comfortably do this long-term. A buzzcut. No lotion, just water on my face. Zero taste or thought put into an outfit. A No Frills bag in my hand, lunchbox inside containing both lunch and dinner for class days. Why would I want anything else? It felt strange that anyone else would find this strange. The only rational explanation is that they’re trying to impress someone — to attract or win over a romantic or business partner — and so all the bizarre, implicit, fleeting standards of a particular culture and age sneak into their decisions. But I’m not trying to do that.

Then a problem emerged. I took the Stoic initiative to subtract from my desires in order to build resilience and independence, and in the process I seemed to lose my motivation to take life seriously — which for me right now means doing my homework, finding internships, getting my degree, and creating wealth. After all, what’s the point of those things if I can manage without them? Working my ass off for a hardcore math or programming course felt pointless, and I struggled to maintain discipline.

I was born comfortably middle-class and have never felt bad about my family’s circumstances, even now. I was an insensitive, autistic, often outright ignorant kid. Neither the lives of blue-collar workers I saw out in the world (which in China means not just dire conditions but being seen, and often addressed explicitly, as the absolute rock bottom of the social hierarchy — something that really fucks with your head and personality), nor the fancy house of the richest kid in my primary-school class, ever got into my head. Maybe if I’d been born into the blue-collar working class, the socially imposed sense of sick inferiority would have driven me to take education seriously in order to one day be somebody. But in the head of this version of me, I don’t find a reason to put in any effort beyond what’s required to keep myself out of poverty.

Even keeping myself out of poverty is something I recognize as important only intellectually, and I think I might not be doing enough — or, in other words, I might actually fail to keep myself out of poverty if I suddenly lost all family support today. What’s acknowledged intellectually doesn’t get internalized as fuel to get me out of bed every morning.

Does this make me sound depressed? Do I lack motivation compared to an average person, regardless of socioeconomic status? I once thought I could engineer my own sense of purpose and source of motivation.

I told myself I needed to strive for financial freedom so I could travel and focus on whatever intellectual, artistic, spiritual, or psychosexual pursuit — but it doesn’t seem to justify the grind.

I told myself an old age defined by pain, sickness, dementia, reliance on others, and probable abuse would be a living hell, in order to push myself to the gym, eat well, and sleep on schedule. But I’m just not actually that afraid — and so not motivated enough to take care of myself seriously, the same way a smoker who isn’t currently trying to quit isn’t seriously afraid of lung cancer and a slow, painful death. You’d think any person should fear a slow, painful death, but most people who don’t take health or wealth seriously simply don’t, even after “thinking it through.” Being able to picture something in the prefrontal cortex doesn’t automatically reach the limbic system or the amygdala. Human minds are like that. Maybe one day I should visit a nursing home to scare myself into preserving my health for real.

But I’m not always attachment-free or motivation-free. Nor is my depressed self functional or healthy. From the age of 10, I was a fat kid. I was at my fattest around 16. Coupled with that, I grew up in 2010s China, where the pop culture and the particular subculture I was engaging in — both online and in person with classmates and friends — idolized male beauty and sexuality. As a result, I have lived, to this day, in dissatisfaction with my own appearance. I always kept my shirt on, even in my all-male dorm room, where a couple of people just as fat were comfortable in their bodies.

I’m always in disgust and quite in despair about myself. From August to December 2023, around my 17th birthday, I lost weight aggressively through cardio and a low-carb diet to the point that I became clinically underweight and developed full-blown anorexia. My weight stabilized, but the anorexia continued until December 2024, when I had my first binge. Since then, until now in May 2026, I’ve never fully recovered. My current BMI is borderline overweight — except this time my face and limbs stayed just as skinny, since the weight from this binge eating disorder, on top of where the anorexia left me, went straight to my abdomen, the body part that made me feel disgusted and despairing about myself in the first place. I once went to the gym consistently, trying to build muscle so I wouldn’t rely on restriction alone to manage my figure — but I fell off. Whenever I strength-trained consistently, I couldn’t eat enough to build proper muscle; whenever I binged, I was too tired, sluggish, and depressed to go to the gym. And not really dating or seeking a partner, I don’t have a strong, positive motivation to look good naked — except my disgust with myself, which, maybe it’s just me, isn’t strong enough to keep me going consistently.

But I trust I can stabilize and one day return to the gym and eventually get fit. I just need to solve the motivation problem. And that brings me back to the Schopenhauer quote at the beginning. You don’t get to motivate yourself through rational means. I believed in the Stoic and psychotherapeutic idea that you can let go of your past and decide your future rationally. In line with those values, I told myself I needed to create wealth and exit options, to become a king-philosopher techno-libertarian sovereign individual focused on health and longevity, with founding startups, investing, quant finance, data science, data engineering, or machine learning engineering as my base career. That framework burned me out and proved unsustainable.

What I keep coming back to — both now, when I have intense career anxieties and school deadlines up my ass, and what will surely come back to haunt me again if I ever achieve financial freedom — is, first, the mysteries and truths of the universe, cultivated by all the universe documentaries and sci-fi I’ve consumed (Wonders of the Universe, The Three-Body Problem, Interstellar), and second — but no less important — to be jacked and sexually desirable.

The deepest anguish of the soul is already chosen for you by fate. I tried to ignore it, deny it, and reconcile it, and none of it worked. Mushrooms, philosophy, watered-down liberal-hippie Western Buddhism, the Roman Catholic Church, the happy-purity evangelical Jesus. What I soon realized is that none of it is, in any sense, more accepting than my peers in my teenage years who bullied me, mocked me, and made me hate myself and cut myself. The only difference is hypocrisy. None of it worked.

So the good side: the deepest longing of my soul, chosen by fate as described above, will be the single most reliable source of motivation to take my own life seriously — if I just let it fully surface and take me over. The danger: these wants are irrational by nature. They will harm me in the long term. Reading or studying pure theory as a career will make me a miserable intellectual who could struggle to pay bills and hold egotistical resentment toward his own students. Beauty and sexiness inevitably deteriorate with age. Few people in history for whom beauty and sexuality were a big part of their identity had a happy ending.

What I should be striving for is freedom through wealth, and subsequently functional health and longevity. But longevity itself — the whole project of Outlive, to compress the “deterioration period” of late life, where chronic illness makes existence miserable, from two decades to three to five years, and in the luckiest cases zero (centenarians who were still working or hanging out doing what they loved a few months, weeks, or even less than a day before they dropped dead) — just feels too far away to consistently motivate a 20-something like me. Alternatively, sacrificial devotion to a family or a cause. But you don’t choose those either. You have a genuine religious or ideological conversion experience, or you meet someone whose presence alone gives you joy, or who gives you a reason to live. You don’t ask for these things, and betting on encountering them is a poor strategy.

If only I could reconcile them. I engage in quant finance because it lets me use high-level math to understand the truths and structure of reality. I work out both to look good and to live long and healthy. But it’s difficult; they are by nature different directions and require different priorities of focus. Trying to satisfy both may mean satisfying neither. How about a math professor who works out to hook up and needs minimal money? But only wealth is the most reliable source of independence and autonomy in old age.

Alternatively, I could make removing and reconciling these existential anguishes my actual goal. It’s possible, but it requires lots of time, money, and effort. Ayahuasca trips, psychedelic experiences, finding true love, psychotherapy, meditation. A 10-day Vipassana retreat (which means being free from a 9-to-5 wage-slave life first). Studying and practicing seriously under a Vajrayana lama (lots of donations usually required — get rich first). But there’s a chance that in the end, it just doesn’t work. From an evolutionary standpoint, it’s not supposed to work.

I just don’t want to live the rest of my days in quiet despair.
